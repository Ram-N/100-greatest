# XX - The Roads to Sata

### *Alan Booth*

**" I was walking away from the south and into the north, and I was walking away from my youth."** - *Alan Booth*

---

### **[ The Essentials ]**

* **Genre:** Travel Memoir
* **Sub-Genre:** Pedestrian Travel, Cultural Criticism
* **Keywords:** *Japan, Walking, Rural Life, 1970s, Outsider, Landscape, Humor, Endurance*
* **Traveler Profile:** **The Cultural Observer.** You want to see the real Japan found in back alleys and village bars rather than glossy brochures.

---

### **[ The Author: Alan Booth ]**

Alan Booth was a tall Englishman who loved Japan but refused to romanticize it. He lived in Tokyo for years and worked in film and theater. He possessed a perfect command of the language. This allowed him to pierce the gaijin bubble. He was a giant moving through a landscape of small spaces. Booth did not care for Zen gardens or silk kimonos. He wanted the Japan of mud and sweat and woodsmoke.

He carried everything on his back and walked for four months. He faced blistering heat and mountains that never seemed to end. His wit was sharp and his observations were often biting. He showed the world a country that was rapidly changing. Booth died young but he left behind a legacy of honest and gritty travel writing. He remains the gold standard for anyone walking through the heart of Japan.

---

### **[ The Book ]**

Booth started at Cape Sata in the south and finished at Cape Soya in the north. It was a journey of 2000 miles on foot. This book is a masterpiece of pedestrian travel. He avoids the tourist traps and sticks to the secondary roads. He drinks cheap sake in tiny inns. He talks to farmers and students and grumpy shopkeepers. The writing is colloquial and full of life. It captures the rhythm of the road.

You can almost feel the blisters on his feet. He explores the tension of being a perpetual outsider. Even with fluent Japanese he is still a stranger. The book is more than a travelogue. It is a portrait of a nation caught between its past and its future. It belongs in the top 100 because it stripped away the myths of Japan. It is honest and funny and deeply human. You get the smell of the rain and the weight of the pack. It is a grueling physical feat turned into a literary triumph.

---

### **[ Destinations Explored ]**

* **Cape Sata:** The rugged southern tip where palm trees sway and the long walk begins.
* **Kyoto:** A quick brush with the ancient capital where history is thick and the crowds are heavy.
* **The Japan Alps:** A brutal climb through the spine of the country where the air turns thin and cold.
* **Cape Soya:** The lonely northern edge where the wind howls and the land finally ends.

### **[ Further Reading ]**

**By Booth:**

* ***Looking for the Lost***: A haunting follow up journey through the forgotten corners of the Japanese countryside.

**In a Similar Vein:**

* ***Hokkaido Highway Blues*** by Will Ferguson: A funny and modern account of hitchhiking across Japan during cherry blossom season.
* ***The Narrow Road to the Deep North*** by Matsuo Basho: The classic 17th century travelogue that inspired generations of Japanese walkers.
